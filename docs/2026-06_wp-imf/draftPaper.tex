\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a model used to analyze the long-term output effects of closing public spending efficiency gaps and of reallocating public spending toward more growth-friendly uses.
The framework integrates expenditure-side fiscal policy and human capital formation within an endogenous growth model.
It builds on three strands of the literature: public infrastructure investment, human capital development, and endogenous growth through technology creation and adoption.
The accumulation of public capital is adjusted for efficiency, so that only a fraction of each unit of spending is converted into productive capital.
The model is parameterized separately for a representative advanced economy and a representative emerging market and developing economy.
Simulations show that reallocating spending toward human capital and infrastructure investment, and closing efficiency gaps, deliver sizable long-run output gains, with effects materializing over different horizons across spending categories and country groups.
Over a 25-year horizon the implied output multipliers on permanent increases in public investment are sizable---roughly 1.5 to 1.7 in advanced economies and 2.6 to 2.9 in emerging market and developing economies---and rise further at longer horizons.

\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Public spending, Spending efficiency, Public investment, Human capital, Endogenous growth, Fiscal multipliers

\bigskip

\section{Introduction and Motivation}
%========================================================================

% --- Intro structure follows Sahm (2021), "Writing Economics Research Papers"
%     (literature/ClaudiaSahm_WritingEcon.pdf): one paragraph per element,
%     contribution-led. See docs note .sped.md > "Writing style".
% [1] MOTIVATION --- why the question matters; set the stage with facts.
Public spending is a central lever of economic policy.
Yet the same headline level of spending can deliver very different economic returns.
With public debt elevated and fiscal space limited in many economies, the quality of spending matters as much as its quantity.
Governments increasingly need their budgets to do more with less.
Two features of public budgets shape their long-run payoff.
The first is \emph{allocation}: how spending is split between current consumption and growth-enhancing investment in infrastructure, human capital, and innovation.
The second is \emph{efficiency}: how much of each dollar spent is actually converted into productive capital, as opposed to being lost to weak project selection, governance gaps, and implementation slippage.

% [2] RESEARCH QUESTION + CONTRIBUTION --- lead with our own contribution, then
%     set it against others in one sentence (substitutes for a lit-review section).
This paper develops and documents a model to answer a specific question: how much can a country raise long-run output by reallocating public spending toward more productive uses and by closing spending-efficiency gaps?
Its contribution is to integrate the entire expenditure side of fiscal policy---infrastructure, human capital, and R\&D---together with household human capital formation within a single endogenous-growth model in which both the allocation and the efficiency of spending are operative.
Where existing work typically isolates one channel---public-investment multipliers, human-capital formation, or innovation---this integration lets the model speak to the horizon over which each type of spending pays off and to how country characteristics shape the gains.

% [3] METHODS --- key features of the approach; the former strand-by-strand lit
%     review folded in here as context (citations kept), not as a separate section.
The model embeds these channels in an otherwise standard New Keynesian framework with endogenous growth.
Public investment builds productive infrastructure capital---roads, communication networks---that raises private-sector productivity, in the tradition of the public-investment and output-multiplier literature \citep{TraumYang2015, DrautzburgUhlig2015, IMF2020FM}.
Households accumulate human capital by devoting time to education and health, complemented by a stock of public human capital such as schools and hospitals \citep{BlankenauSimpson2004, Dissouetal2016, Atoliaetal2021}.
Growth is endogenous: following \citet{CominGertler2006} and \citet{Anzoateguietal2019}, the model separates the stock of created technologies $Z_t$, available for adoption, from the stock of adopted technologies $A_t$ in use, so that public R\&D raises $Z_t$ while costly private adoption expands $A_t$ and the gap $Z_t-A_t$ generates realistic diffusion lags.
Across all three channels, public spending is efficiency-adjusted \citep{Bergetal2019}: only a fraction of each unit spent is converted into productive capital.
The model is calibrated separately for a representative advanced economy and a representative emerging market and developing economy.

% [4] RESULTS --- the payoff; at most three findings, stated with numbers. Make them shine.
The main findings are threefold.
First, reallocating spending toward investment raises output over the long run, with infrastructure paying off sooner and human capital later.
Second, closing efficiency gaps delivers sizable additional gains, the more so for economies that start further from the efficiency frontier.
Third, the gains are larger for emerging market and developing economies, reflecting a higher marginal product of public capital.
Over a 25-year horizon the implied output multipliers on permanent spending increases are about 1.5 for infrastructure and 1.7 for human capital in advanced economies.
The corresponding multipliers are larger in emerging market and developing economies, at about 2.6 and 2.9, respectively.
The advanced-economy R\&D multiplier is about 2.4, and all rise further at longer horizons.

\section{The Model} \label{sec:model}
%========================================================================

The model is built on a relatively standard New Keynesian framework augmented with endogenous growth. 
Its central extension, for the purpose of analyzing public spending, is the introduction of productive public spending components: investment in physical infrastructure, investment in human capital, and spending on research and development. 
These outlays build, respectively, a stock of public infrastructure capital (e.g., roads and bridges), public  utilities facilitating the accumulation of human capital (e.g., schools and hospitals), and the economy's stock of technological knowledge (e.g., publicly funded patents not yet adopted by the private sector).
All are created from efficiency-adjusted public spending, recognizing that a fraction of each flow is wasted through inefficiency.


The economy contains a representative household, a multi-tiered production sector, and a government.
The household chooses consumption, labor supply, private investment, holdings of government debt, and---departing from the standard framework---time devoted to human capital accumulation.
The production sector contains a competitive final-good producer, retailers that set prices subject to Calvo frictions, intermediate producers that operate Cobb--Douglas technologies, and adopters that convert created but unadopted technologies into usable ones.
The government comprises a fiscal authority and a central bank.
The fiscal authority spends on government consumption, public infrastructure, public human capital, and public R\&D, in addition to lump-sum transfers, and levies taxes on consumption and labor income.
The central bank sets the nominal interest rate according to a Taylor rule.

The economy grows along a balanced-growth path driven by technology; the trend level is denoted $\Lambda_t$ and grows at the constant gross rate $g$.
The remainder of this section sets out the model's building blocks in turn, starting from households.
The derivations for the household, production, pricing, and technology-adoption conditions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:equilibrium}; Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} reports the stationarized model equations and notation.

\subsection{Households}

The representative household maximizes expected lifetime utility,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:utility}
    \mathbb{E}_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t \left[\ln C_t - \omega \frac{\left(L_t + E_t\right)^{1+\varphi}}{1+\varphi}\right],
\end{equation}
where $C_t$ is consumption, $\beta \in (0,1)$ is the discount factor, $\varphi$ is the inverse Frisch elasticity, and $\omega$ scales the disutility of time spent away from leisure.
The household's unit time endowment is split between labor supply $L_t$, time devoted to human capital accumulation $E_t$, and leisure $1 - L_t - E_t$.
Because work and education enter the same time-cost term, the household trades off the current return to labor against the future return to human-capital accumulation.
The flow budget constraint, in real terms, is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:hhbudget}
    \left(1+\tau_t^{c}\right) C_t + I_t + B_t = \left(1-\tau_t^{w}\right) w_t N_t + r_t^{k} K_{t-1} + \frac{R_{t-1}}{\Pi_t} B_{t-1} + T_t + \Omega_t,
\end{equation}
where $\tau_t^{c}$ and $\tau_t^{w}$ are the consumption and labor income tax rates, $I_t$ is private investment, $B_t$ is real holdings of government debt paying the gross return $R_{t-1}$, $\Pi_t$ is gross inflation, $w_t$ is the real wage, $N_t \equiv H_{t-1} L_t$ is effective labor, $r_t^{k}$ is the rental rate of private capital, $T_t$ are lump-sum transfers, and $\Omega_t$ are rebated profits.
The stock variables that generate current income, $K_{t-1}$ and $H_{t-1}$, are predetermined at date $t$.

Let $\lambda_t$ denote the marginal value of real wealth.
The household's standard saving margins imply
\begin{align}
    \lambda_t &= \frac{1}{\left(1+\tau_t^{c}\right) C_t}, \label{eq:mu}\\
    \lambda_t &= \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\lambda_{t+1}\frac{R_t}{\Pi_{t+1}}\right], \label{eq:euler}\\
    \lambda_t &= \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\lambda_{t+1}\left(r_{t+1}^{k} + 1 - \delta\right)\right], \label{eq:capital}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:mu} links the marginal value of wealth to the tax-adjusted marginal utility of consumption, while equations~\eqref{eq:euler} and \eqref{eq:capital} are the bond and private-capital Euler equations.
Private capital accumulates according to
\begin{equation} \label{eq:privcapitalacc}
    K_t = (1-\delta) K_{t-1} + I_t,
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is the depreciation rate.

The main household extension is the human-capital accumulation channel, in which private time devoted to education and health is complemented by public human-capital investment \citep{BlankenauSimpson2004, Dissouetal2016, Atoliaetal2021}.
Private human capital $H_t$ evolves according to
\begin{equation} \label{eq:humancapital}
    H_{t} = \left(1 - \delta^{h}\right) H_{t-1} + \chi \left(E_t\right)^{\gamma} \left(\frac{K_{t-1}^{GE}}{\Lambda_{t-1}}\right)^{\mu},
\end{equation}
Here $\delta^{h}$ is the depreciation rate of human capital and $\chi$ is a scale constant determined from the accumulation equation to ensure the normalized steady-state value $H=1$.
The stock $K_t^{GE}$ represents public education and health capital, while expressing the inherited stock $K_{t-1}^{GE}$ relative to $\Lambda_{t-1}$ keeps its contribution stationary along the balanced-growth path.
The elasticities $\gamma$ and $\mu$ govern the response of human capital formation to the household's time input and to the public human capital stock, respectively.
Public human capital therefore raises the productivity of household education time, rather than entering household utility directly.
Letting $\lambda_t^{H}$ be the shadow value of human capital, the labor and education-time conditions are
\begin{align}
    \omega \left(L_t + E_t\right)^{\varphi} &= \lambda_t \left(1-\tau_t^{w}\right) w_t H_{t-1}, \label{eq:labor}\\
    \omega \left(L_t + E_t\right)^{\varphi} &= \lambda_t^{H}\, \chi \gamma \left(E_t\right)^{\gamma-1} \left(\frac{K_{t-1}^{GE}}{\Lambda_{t-1}}\right)^{\mu}, \label{eq:edutime}\\
    \lambda_t^{H} &= \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\lambda_{t+1}\left(1-\tau_{t+1}^{w}\right) w_{t+1} L_{t+1} + \lambda_{t+1}^{H}\left(1-\delta^{h}\right)\right]. \label{eq:hcvalue}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:labor} values an additional hour of work at the after-tax wage in efficiency units.
Equation~\eqref{eq:edutime} values an additional hour of education by the human capital it creates, and equation~\eqref{eq:hcvalue} prices human capital as the discounted value of future labor income plus the undepreciated continuation stock.

\subsection{Production and Technology}

The production side departs from a textbook New Keynesian framework along several dimensions.
First, it separates price setting from real production: retailers set sticky prices, while intermediate producers determine unit factor cost and sell to retailers at a fixed gross markup.
In this structure, unit factor cost and retail marginal cost are not equivalent; the wedge between them generates residual profits that accrue to the owners of adopted technologies.
Second, production depends on human capital via effective labor, $N_t = H_{t-1}L_t$, rather than hours alone, and public infrastructure directly shifts productive capacity.
Finally, productivity evolves endogenously through a creation-adoption technology block in which public R\&D expands the stock of ideas and private adoption turns them into productive technologies.

\paragraph{Retail firms.}
In the textbook setup, intermediate-good producers both operate the production technology and set sticky prices.
Here, these roles are split.
The framework introduces retailers, which purchase intermediate output at the relative price $mc_t$, transform it one-for-one into differentiated varieties, and set prices subject to Calvo frictions.
Conditional on $mc_t$, retailers face the standard New Keynesian Calvo pricing block.
The final-good aggregation block is standard as well.
Appendix~\ref{app:equilibrium} contains the final-good and pricing equations.
Intermediate-good firms are therefore responsible only for real production.

\paragraph{Production and marginal cost.}
Intermediate producers use private capital and effective labor, taking public infrastructure and adopted technology as external production shifters.
In symmetric equilibrium, aggregate production is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:production}
    Y_t = A_{t-1}^{\vartheta-1} K_{t-1}^{\alpha} N_t^{1-\alpha} \left(K_{t-1}^{GI}\right)^{\alpha_G},
\end{equation}
where $A_{t-1}$ is the stock of adopted technologies, $K_{t-1}$ is private capital, $N_t = H_{t-1}L_t$ is effective labor, and $K_{t-1}^{GI}$ is the stock of public infrastructure capital.
Public infrastructure augments private production as a positive externality, while adopted technologies raise productivity through the love-of-variety term $A_{t-1}^{\vartheta-1}$.
The parameter $\vartheta$ governs the productivity impact of technology adoption, while $\alpha$ and $\alpha_G$ denote the output elasticities of private capital and public infrastructure, respectively.\footnote{In \citet{Anzoateguietal2019} a single intermediate-goods elasticity of substitution sets both the markup and the returns to variety.
Here the retail markup is governed by $\epsilon$ and the intermediate producers' markup by the separate parameter $\mu^{p}$; $\vartheta$ retains only the returns-to-variety role, mapping the stock of adopted technologies into TFP through the love-of-variety term $A_t^{\vartheta-1}$.}

The cost-minimization problem leads to familiar optimality conditions.
The only deviation is that the unit factor cost equals the selling price $mc_t$ divided by the fixed gross markup $\mu^{p}$, which gives rise to $\frac{mc_t}{\mu^{p}}$ in the equations below.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:factordemand}
    r_t^{k} = \alpha\, \frac{mc_t}{\mu^{p}} \frac{Y_t}{K_{t-1}}, \qquad w_t = (1-\alpha)\, \frac{mc_t}{\mu^{p}} \frac{Y_t}{N_t}, \qquad \frac{K_{t-1}}{N_t} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{w_t}{r_t^{k}}.
\end{equation}
The markup wedge leaves the residual profit flow $\frac{\mu^{p}-1}{\mu^{p}}\, mc_t\, Y_t$, which enters the following technology adoption problem.
The intermediate-good sector equations are derived in Appendix~\ref{app:equilibrium}.

\paragraph{Technology creation and adoption.}
Following \citet{CominGertler2006} and \citet{Anzoateguietal2019}, the model distinguishes between the stock of created technologies $Z_t$, available for adoption, and the stock of adopted technologies $A_t$ that enters production.
The gap $Z_t - A_t$ is the stock of unadopted technologies.
Created technologies depend on two forces.

First, lagged human capital raises technology creation through a reduced-form learning-by-doing channel.
This specification borrows the persistent learning-by-doing form of \citet{ChangGomesSchorfheide2002} and applies it here to innovation.
Second, public R\&D spending $G_t^{RD}$ expands the stock of technologies available for adoption by raising the supply of adoptable ideas.
Technology creation is governed by the following equation:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Z}
\begin{aligned}
    \log\!\left(\frac{Z_t}{Z^{SS}}\right) ={}& \rho_A \log\!\left(\frac{Z_{t-1}}{Z^{SS}}\right)
    + \left(1-\rho_A\right)\alpha_{HA} \log\!\left(\frac{H_{t-1}}{H^{SS}}\right) \\
    &{}+ \left(1-\rho_A\right)\alpha_{RD} \log\!\left(\frac{\left(1-e_{t-1}^{GRD}\right) G_{t-1}^{RD}}{\left(1-e^{GRD}\right) G^{RD,SS}}\right),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Only the productive fraction $\left(1-e_{t-1}^{GRD}\right)$ of (lagged) R\&D spending drives innovation: $e_t^{GRD}$ is the R\&D inefficiency wedge, equal to its steady-state value $e^{GRD}$ in the baseline and reduced when the R\&D efficiency gap is closed (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
The raw spending $G_t^{RD}$ enters the government budget constraint~\eqref{eq:govbudget}.
The coefficients $\alpha_{HA}$ and $\alpha_{RD}$ are the long-run elasticities of created technology with respect to human capital and effective public R\&D, while multiplication by $1-\rho_A$ gives their per-period loadings.

Creation does not raise productivity until technologies are adopted.
To capture this diffusion lag, the stock of adopted technologies evolves as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:A}
    A_{t} = q_t\, \phi \left[Z_{t-1} - A_{t-1}\right] + \phi A_{t-1}.
\end{equation}
The survival rate $\phi$ applies to both adopted and unadopted technologies, since an idea may become obsolete in either state.
The time-varying adoption probability is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:qmain}
    q_t = \left(q_0+\varepsilon_t^q\right)\left(\frac{S_t}{\Lambda_t}\right)^{\varsigma},
\end{equation}
which increases with adopters' trend-adjusted expenditure $S_t/\Lambda_t$ with elasticity $\varsigma$.
The shock $\varepsilon_t^q$ shifts the adoption probability directly and is used only in the technology-diffusion experiments.

Competitive adopters choose $S_t$ to maximize the value of converting unadopted technologies into usable ones, equating the marginal cost of adoption effort to its discounted marginal value.
In stationary units, let $\mathcal{V}_t$ and $\mathcal{J}_t$ denote the aggregate values of adopted and unadopted technologies, respectively, $\mathit{SDF}_{t+1}$ the household stochastic discount factor, and $1+\gamma^a$ gross technology-trend growth.
The resulting optimality condition is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Sfocmain}
    S_t = \varsigma\, q_t\, \phi\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\mathit{SDF}_{t+1}\,
    \frac{1}{1+\gamma^{a}}\, \frac{A_{t-1}}{A_t}
    \left(\mathcal{V}_{t+1}-\mathcal{J}_{t+1}\right)\right].
\end{equation}

Thus, adoption expenditure rises with the elasticity and level of the adoption probability, the survival rate, and the discounted value gain from converting an unadopted technology.
The value functions and derivation of equation~\eqref{eq:Sfocmain} are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:equilibrium}.
The productivity payoff of the adopted-technology stock $A_t$ is realized in the production function~\eqref{eq:production}, with strength governed by $\vartheta$.

\subsection{Government: Fiscal and Monetary Policy}

The fiscal authority determines the composition of public spending and how it is financed while public debt is stabilized.
It can also improve the productivity of spending by narrowing the efficiency gaps, which measure the fraction of investment and R\&D outlays not converted into productive capital or technology.
The monetary authority sets the nominal interest rate faced by the government.

\paragraph{Government budget.}
Let $B_t$ denote real public debt and $b_t=B_t/Y_t$ the debt-to-GDP ratio.
Current debt equals the real value of inherited debt plus the primary deficit:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:govbudget}
    B_t = \frac{R_{t-1}}{\Pi_t} B_{t-1} + G_t^{C} + I_t^{GI} + I_t^{GE} + G_t^{RD} + T_t - \tau_t^{w} w_t N_t - \tau_t^{c} C_t,
\end{equation}
where $R_{t-1}/\Pi_t$ is the realized gross real return on inherited debt.
The primary spending terms are government consumption $G_t^C$, infrastructure investment $I_t^{GI}$, education and health investment $I_t^{GE}$, public R\&D $G_t^{RD}$, and lump-sum transfers $T_t$.
Revenues come from taxes on labor income, $\tau_t^w w_tN_t$, and consumption, $\tau_t^cC_t$.
All spending enters the budget constraint at its full fiscal cost, irrespective of how much becomes productive.

\paragraph{Spending instruments and efficiency.}
The four spending instruments, indexed by $x\in\{C,GI,GE,RD\}$, can be moved independently through their respective expenditure shocks.
Each instrument $X_t$ follows a simple rule under which it deviates from its steady-state level by an innovation scaled to steady-state aggregate demand:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:spendingrule}
    X_t = X^{SS} + Y^{d,SS}\varepsilon_t^x, \qquad x\in\{C,GI,GE,RD\}.
\end{equation}
The persistence and duration of $\varepsilon_t^x$ are determined by the experiment.
Government consumption uses resources but does not build a productive stock.
Infrastructure investment builds public infrastructure capital $K_t^{GI}$, education and health investment builds public education and health capital $K_t^{GE}$, and public R\&D expands the stock of technologies available for adoption.
For the two investment instruments, productive capital evolves according to
\begin{align}
    K_t^{GI} &= \left(1 - \delta^{GI}\right) K_{t-1}^{GI} + \left(1 - e_t^{GI}\right) I_t^{GI}, \label{eq:kgi} \\
    K_t^{GE} &= \left(1 - \delta^{GE}\right) K_{t-1}^{GE} + \left(1 - e_t^{GE}\right) I_t^{GE}. \label{eq:kge}
\end{align}
The parameters $\delta^{GI}$ and $\delta^{GE}$ are the respective depreciation rates, while $e_t^{GI}$ and $e_t^{GE}$ are spending-efficiency gaps.
Only the fraction $1-e_t^x$ of an investment flow becomes productive capital, so closing a gap raises capital formation without increasing expenditure.
The R\&D gap operates analogously: only the productive fraction $\left(1-e_{t-1}^{GRD}\right)G_{t-1}^{RD}$ enters technology creation in equation~\eqref{eq:Z}.

\paragraph{Tax rules, fiscal closure, and experiment financing.}
The consumption- and labor-income-tax rates equal their steady-state values unless perturbed by their respective tax-rate shocks:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:taxrules}
    \tau_t^j = \tau^j + \varepsilon_t^{\tau j}, \qquad j\in\{c,w\}.
\end{equation}
The exercises in this paper set both shocks to zero.
Tax rates do not respond to debt; instead, lump-sum transfers ensure debt stability according to
\begin{equation} \label{eq:transferrule}
    T_t = T^{SS}
    - \gamma_d^T e_{T,t}^{\mathrm{aux}}
    \left(b_{t-1}-b^*\right)Y^{d,SS}.
\end{equation}
Here $e_{T,t}^{\mathrm{aux}}$ is an auxiliary dummy that equals zero throughout the debt-financed model-properties exercises in Section~\ref{sec:properties}, switching off the transfer response to debt for the full spending-shock path.
It returns to one afterward, when the positive coefficient $\gamma_d^T$ makes transfers fall in response to debt above target and thereby restores debt stability.
The policy experiments in Section~\ref{sec:results} instead keep $e_{T,t}^{\mathrm{aux}}=1$ and reallocate spending permanently toward a productive category through an equal reduction in government consumption, making primary spending ex ante budget-neutral.

\paragraph{Monetary policy and the government financing rate.}
The central bank sets the nominal interest rate $R_t$, which is also the government's financing rate, according to a Taylor rule:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:taylor}
    \frac{R_t}{R^{SS}} = \left(\frac{R_{t-1}}{R^{SS}}\right)^{\rho_R}\left[\left(\frac{\Pi_t}{\Pi}\right)^{\gamma_\pi}\left(\frac{mc_t}{mc^{SS}}\right)^{\gamma_y}\right]^{1-\rho_R} e^{\varepsilon_t^{mp}}.
\end{equation}
Here $\rho_R$ is a smoothing parameter, and $\gamma_\pi$ governs the response to inflation.
Following \citet{GertlerKaradi2011}, minus the log deviation of the retail price markup proxies for the flex-price output gap.
Since this markup is $1/mc_t$, the proxy is $\log(mc_t/mc^{SS})$, with response coefficient $\gamma_y$.

\subsection{Market Clearing and Equilibrium}

Final demand is allocated across private consumption and investment, the four public-spending categories, and technology-adoption costs:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:resource}
    Y_t^{d} = C_t + I_t + G_t^{C} + I_t^{GI} + I_t^{GE} + G_t^{RD} + \mathcal{R}_t,
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{R}_t$ is the aggregate resource cost of adoption, equal in stationary units to
\[
    \mathcal{R}_t=\left(\frac{Z_{t-1}}{A_{t-1}}-1\right)S_t.
\]
This is the per-unadopted-technology outlay $S_t$ scaled by the stock of unadopted technologies relative to adopted ones.
Technology creation uses the separate public R\&D flow $G_t^{RD}$ already included in equation~\eqref{eq:resource}.
As in a standard New Keynesian model with Calvo price setting, the price-dispersion index $v_t^{p}$ makes the gross production required to satisfy a given level of final demand exceed that demand, so $Y_t = v_t^{p}Y_t^{d}$.
A competitive equilibrium is a sequence of quantities and prices such that households, producers, retailers, and adopters optimize; fiscal and monetary policy rules hold; and the goods, labor, capital, and bond markets clear for given paths of the exogenous processes.

\subsection{Balanced Growth and Solution} \label{subsec:bgp}

The economy follows a balanced-growth path driven by the technology trend $\Lambda_t$, which grows at the constant gross rate $g$.
The equations above are written mainly in levels to make their economic content transparent, whereas Dynare solves a stationary representation of the system.

Consumption, output, the real wage, private and public capital, government spending, debt, and transfers share the trend $\Lambda_t$.
For a typical date-$t$ quantity $X_t$, define its stationary counterpart as $\hat{X}_t=X_t/\Lambda_t$.
Investment is instead divided by the incumbent trend $\Lambda_{t-1}$, placing the flow in the same units as the predetermined capital stock it augments.
Marginal utility and the technology-value objects move inversely with the scale of the economy, so their stationary counterparts remove the corresponding inverse trend.

Because the trend grows at the constant gross rate $g$, these transformations leave the factor $g=\Lambda_t/\Lambda_{t-1}$ wherever an equation links trending variables dated in different periods.
For example, the level bond Euler equation $\lambda_t = \beta\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\lambda_{t+1}R_t/\Pi_{t+1}\right]$ becomes
\[
    \hat{\lambda}_t = \beta\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\hat{\lambda}_{t+1}\frac{R_t}{g\Pi_{t+1}}\right],
\]
while the private-capital law $K_t=(1-\delta)K_{t-1}+I_t$ becomes
\[
    g\hat{K}_t=(1-\delta)\hat{K}_{t-1}+\hat{I}_t.
\]
Thus accumulation equations carry the growth factor on the current stock, while Euler and asset-pricing conditions carry it in the intertemporal discount term.

The created- and adopted-technology stocks are the exception.
They grow at their own gross rate $1+\gamma^{a}=g^{(1-\alpha)/(\vartheta-1)}$, which is faster than output growth because technology enters production through the love-of-variety term $A_t^{\vartheta-1}$.
Their laws of motion and value functions therefore carry $1+\gamma^{a}$ rather than $g$.
For this reason, the technology block in Appendix~\ref{app:equilibrium} is stated directly in stationarized form, with adoption expenditure $S_t$ also expressed relative to the trend.
The steady state of the stationary system---with hats suppressed in Appendix~\ref{app:equilibrium} and in the Dynare files---corresponds to the balanced-growth path of the level economy.

The model is implemented in Dynare and solved as a deterministic, perfect-foresight system.
The policy experiments in Section~\ref{sec:results} are permanent reforms introduced in 2025, with responses traced out to 2050 around the balanced-growth steady state.

\section{Calibration} \label{sec:calibration}
%========================================================================

The model is calibrated at a quarterly frequency, separately for a representative advanced economy (AE) and a representative emerging market and developing economy (EMDE).
The calibration is organized around parameters common to both country groups, country-group-specific steady-state targets, and structural parameters governing the productive-spending and endogenous-growth channels.
Spending-efficiency gaps are treated separately, as are scale parameters calibrated internally to match steady-state targets and coefficients derived from equilibrium restrictions.
The main text reports the choices most relevant to the quantitative exercises; Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} gives the complete parameter vector and its model-code mapping.

\subsection{Calibration Strategy and Common Parameters}

The structure of the New Keynesian core follows \citet{gali2015}, with its parameters calibrated at conventional quarterly values.
The standard extensions for capital accumulation and price indexation are benchmarked to the IMF's Global Integrated Monetary and Fiscal (GIMF) model \citep{Andersonetal2013}.\footnote{Because GIMF has a richer sectoral structure and uses inflation-adjustment costs rather than the compact Calvo block adopted here, it guides these calibration choices rather than providing a coefficient-by-coefficient mapping.}
The production extension, which introduces a gross markup for intermediate producers and productivity gains from a larger stock of adopted technologies, follows \citet{Anzoateguietal2019}.
The parameters described below are held common across the AE and EMDE calibrations and summarized in Table~\ref{tab:commonParameters}.

For households and capital accumulation, the quarterly discount factor is $\beta=0.9985$, and the private-capital share in production is $\alpha=0.30$.
The inverse Frisch elasticity is $\varphi=5$, corresponding to a Frisch elasticity of 0.2.
Private capital, public physical capital, and human capital all depreciate at the same quarterly rate, $\delta=\delta^h=0.025$.

Under Calvo pricing, the probability that a retailer cannot reset its price is $\theta_p=0.80$, implying an average price duration of five quarters, and non-reset prices are indexed to past inflation with coefficient $\chi_p=0.60$.
The elasticity of substitution across retail goods is $\epsilon=10$.
Intermediate producers charge a gross markup of $\mu^p=1.18$, and the love-of-variety exponent is $\vartheta=1.35$.
Anzoategui et al.\ place the markup in the middle of the range of available estimates and choose the variety parameter to imply an elasticity of substitution of 3.85, in line with the product-level estimates of \citet{BrodaWeinstein2006}.
In this model, the two parameters play distinct roles: $\mu^p$ determines the profit flow that gives adopted technologies value, while $\vartheta$ governs the productivity gain from a larger stock of adopted technologies.

Steady-state gross inflation is normalized to $\Pi=1$.
The monetary-policy rule uses representative quarterly coefficients: interest-rate smoothing is $\rho_R=0.70$, and the response to inflation is $\gamma_\pi=1.50$.
Following \citet{GertlerKaradi2011}, the response to the markup-based output-gap proxy is $\gamma_y=0.125$, corresponding to their quarterly coefficient of $0.50/4$.
Fiscal closure operates through lump-sum transfers, with $\gamma_d^T=0.01$ governing their response to the lagged debt gap.
The auxiliary switch temporarily suspends this response, ensuring that the spending expansions in the model-properties exercises are financed entirely through debt.

\subsection{Productive-Spending and Endogenous-Growth Parameters} \label{subsec:productive-calibration}

Each productive-spending category operates through a distinct propagation mechanism, and the country-specific calibration reflects differences in initial productive stocks and innovation capacity.
Infrastructure investment builds public capital; human-capital investment raises workforce skills.
Because both stocks are less developed in EMDEs, the associated elasticities are larger than in AEs.
The technology block differs in nature: AEs feature endogenous technology creation through human capital and public R\&D, whereas EMDEs adopt from an exogenously expanding technology frontier.
Adoption remains endogenous in both calibrations, but is less responsive to expenditure in EMDEs.

The elasticity of human-capital accumulation with respect to the public human-capital stock, $\mu$, is 0.25 for EMDEs and 0.1 for AEs, within the range documented in \citet{GlommRavikumar1998}, \citet{Blankenau2005}, and \citet{Atoliaetal2021}.

For EMDEs, the created-technology stock instead follows an exogenous balanced-growth path with gross growth rate $1+\gamma^a$.
Within the AE calibration, $\rho_A$ is set at 0.79 quarterly, following the persistence estimate in \citet{ChangGomesSchorfheide2002}.
Given this persistence, the human-capital elasticity reported in Table~\ref{tab:parameters} translates into a per-quarter loading of $(1-\rho_A)\alpha_{HA}=0.10$ for the learning-by-doing channel.
The public R\&D elasticity similarly translates into a per-quarter loading of $(1-\rho_A)\alpha_{RD}=0.0189$, remaining close to the R\&D impact estimates in \citet{FieldhouseMertens2023}.

Technology adoption is endogenous and follows the same structure in both calibrations.
Following \citet{CominGertler2006} and \citet{Anzoateguietal2019}, the quarterly technology-survival rate is $\phi=0.98$ for both country groups, corresponding to an annual obsolescence rate of about 8 percent.
The elasticity of the adoption probability with respect to adoption spending, $\varsigma$, is set at 0.8 for AEs and 0.1 for EMDEs.
The lower EMDE value preserves endogenous diffusion of the existing stock of unadopted technologies while making the adoption probability less responsive to adoption expenditure.

\subsection{Steady-State Coefficients and Experiment Design}

Three scale parameters are solved jointly with the steady state rather than selected directly, allowing the model to implement its steady-state targets, normalizations, and optimality conditions.
Following a standard normalization in quarterly DSGE models, as in \citet{Cantoreetal2017}, steady-state labor supply equals one-third of the available time endowment, $L=1/3$.
The labor-disutility scale $\omega$ is determined internally to implement this target.
The household optimality conditions imply $E=0.157$ given $L=1/3$ and the calibrated values of $\beta$, $\delta^h$, and $\gamma$.
Conditional on this allocation, the human-capital accumulation scale $\chi$ is determined from the accumulation equation to ensure the normalized steady-state value $H=1$.
Following \citet{Anzoateguietal2019}, the steady-state adoption probability is set to $q^{SS}=0.05$ per quarter.
This target implies an average adoption lag of $1/q^{SS}=20$ quarters, or five years.
The scale $q_0$ in $q_t=q_0(S_t/\Lambda_t)^{\varsigma}$ is determined internally so that the adoption-probability equation attains this target in the steady state.

Whereas the three scale parameters are determined within the steady state, the technology-growth rate is implied by trend output growth according to $1+\gamma^a=g^{(1-\alpha)/(\vartheta-1)}$.
At zero steady-state inflation, the steady-state nominal interest rate satisfies $R^{SS}=g/\beta$.
Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} reports the resulting values of the internally calibrated scales and all derived steady-state objects.
The size, duration, and persistence of the exercises described in Sections~\ref{sec:properties} and~\ref{sec:results} are specified through the paths of the relevant exogenous shocks.

\section{Model Properties} \label{sec:properties}
%========================================================================

This section documents the model's quantitative properties before turning to the policy reforms of Section~\ref{sec:results}.
It first traces the responses to wasteful government consumption and to three productive instruments: infrastructure investment, human-capital investment, and public R\&D.
It then examines their sensitivity to key structural parameters, summarizes them as cumulative fiscal multipliers, and traces the effects of improving spending efficiency.
All exercises consider permanent changes.
Appendix~\ref{app:tempshocks} reports the corresponding results for persistent but temporary shocks.

The transmission analysis focuses on the AE calibration to describe the underlying mechanisms clearly.
The sensitivity analysis then varies structural parameters central to the calibration differences between the two groups.
The cumulative-multiplier analysis subsequently adds the EMDE calibration, allowing the responses to be compared across country groups.

\subsection{Transmission of Public Spending} \label{subsec:transmission}

The standard-shock exercise raises each spending instrument permanently by ex-ante 1 percent of GDP, with no offsetting spending cut or tax increase.
The expansions are entirely debt financed throughout the reported horizon by setting the auxiliary dummy $e_{T,t}^{\mathrm{aux}}$ to zero.
This suspends the transfer-rule response to debt.
Figure~\ref{fig:standardShocks} reports the resulting AE impulse responses, with panels arranged by demand, productive stocks, labor, prices and rates, and fiscal blocks.

\emph{Government consumption} provides a useful benchmark because it is the standard fiscal instrument in macroeconomic analyses of government-spending shocks.
As a component of aggregate demand, its permanent increase raises total output.
At the same time, forward-looking households anticipate that the additional debt will eventually require lower lump-sum transfers.
The resulting negative wealth effect crowds out private consumption and raises labor supply.
The latter supports output even though government consumption does not add directly to productive capacity.
Setting aside the initial adjustment, the output gain reaches around one-half of a percent by the end of the horizon shown.
Turning to price dynamics, the inherited capital stock cannot adjust on impact, so higher demand initially raises labor relative to capital and pushes up real marginal cost.
Calvo price setters pass this increase into inflation.
The monetary-policy rule raises the nominal interest rate in response to both inflation and the markup-based proxy for the flex-price output gap.\footnote{Appendix~\ref{app:simplified} shows that inflation disappears exactly when private capital and its factor-demand conditions are removed; in the resulting labor-only model, the permanent expansion leaves real marginal cost and inflation unchanged.}
On the fiscal side, financing the spending increase entirely through debt widens the primary deficit and causes public debt to accumulate sharply throughout the reported horizon.

The productive instruments share the demand, price, and debt-financing channels described for wasteful government consumption.
All instruments are components of aggregate demand and, setting aside the endogenous responses of other variables, an increase in any one of them mechanically raises output.
Their distinguishing effects arise from the assets they create and how quickly those assets become productive.

\emph{Infrastructure investment} builds public capital from the outset.
Because this stock enters production directly through equation~\eqref{eq:production}, it raises productivity sooner than the other productive instruments and eventually crowds in private investment and consumption.
Although the labor-supply response is of a similar order of magnitude to that under government consumption, the output gain is substantially larger.
By the end of the horizon shown, output is around 2.5 percent above its steady-state level, compared with around one-half of a percent under government consumption.
The greater pressure on the inherited capital stock produces a larger impact increase in real marginal cost and inflation than under government consumption.
The gains in economic activity also broaden the tax bases, gradually narrowing the primary deficit and slowing debt accumulation relative to government consumption.

\emph{Human-capital investment} adds another supply channel.
It builds public education and health capital, which in turn complements the time households devote to education and health through equation~\eqref{eq:humancapital}.
Although public education and health capital begins accumulating immediately, its effects on human capital and effective labor take time to materialize.
This investment therefore takes considerably longer to realize its full productive potential.
Human-capital investment is nevertheless particularly potent in raising output.
In the short run, it induces households to reallocate some time from education to work, providing an immediate boost to labor supply.
In the long run, households provide more effective labor through accumulated human capital rather than longer working hours.
By the end of the horizon, output is more than 4 percent above its steady-state level, while consumption and private investment have both risen above their steady-state levels.
Inflation initially rises by less than under infrastructure investment because price setters anticipate the subsequent decline in real marginal cost.
It strengthens as output takes off later in the horizon.
The larger tax base nearly closes the primary deficit by that point, although the legacy debt remains substantial.

\emph{Public R\&D} involves a two-stage supply channel.
It first expands the stock of technologies available for adoption, after which private adoption converts them into technologies used in production (equations~\ref{eq:Z} and~\ref{eq:A}).
Because this instrument does not quickly add to productive capacity, output briefly dips while new technologies are being adopted.
Because the productive benefits are delayed while spending continues, demand pressures persist and inflation becomes the strongest among all instruments for many years.
What follows, however, is a powerful boost to output, which rises to around 4.5 percent above its steady-state level by the end of the horizon.
The resulting gains in economic activity benefit public finances, eliminating the primary deficit by the end of the reported horizon.
Public debt is consequently lower than under the other spending increases.

\subsection{Sensitivity}

The productive-spending elasticities are central to both the size of the output gains and the differences between the two country-group calibrations.
In the model, the strength of each productive-spending channel is governed primarily by its own elasticity.
The infrastructure response scales with the output elasticity of public infrastructure ($\alpha_G$).
The human-capital response scales with the elasticity of human-capital formation with respect to the public stock ($\mu$).
The R\&D response scales with the elasticity of technology creation with respect to public R\&D ($\alpha_{RD}$).

Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} illustrates this sensitivity by tracing the output response to each permanent productive expansion as its relevant elasticity is varied over an illustrative range.
At the 25-year horizon, the output response spans about 1.3 to 8.4 percent for infrastructure, 2.4 to 12.6 percent for human capital, and 1.9 to 7.4 percent for R\&D over the parameter ranges considered.
The timing of the gains is robust across these ranges.
Infrastructure begins to pay off earliest and then builds gradually.
Human-capital investment produces a short-lived impact gain before larger benefits emerge over time.
R\&D briefly lowers output after impact and delivers its strongest gains later.

The highlighted country-group values show how the calibration of these elasticities contributes to differences in the effects of productive spending.
The values are grounded in the literature reviewed in Subsection~\ref{subsec:productive-calibration} and capture relevant structural differences between the two groups.
At the EMDE values of $\alpha_G$ and $\mu$, infrastructure and human-capital investment are more potent than at the corresponding AE values.

\subsection{Fiscal Multipliers}

Cumulative fiscal multipliers complement the impulse responses by expressing the discounted output gain per unit of additional spending.
Their evolution across horizons reveals a central feature of the model: demand effects materialize quickly, whereas productive gains build over time as public capital, human capital, and technologies accumulate.
Because the transmission analysis in Subsection~\ref{subsec:transmission} focuses on AEs, the multiplier comparison also shows how the effects differ for EMDEs.

Each multiplier is computed from the debt-financed permanent increases analyzed in Subsection~\ref{subsec:transmission}.
One spending category rises by 1 percent of GDP, with no offsetting cut to other spending.\footnote{Appendix~\ref{app:tempshocks} reports the persistent AR(1) counterparts, which are generally larger at intermediate horizons.}
Output and spending are discounted at the model's steady-state real interest rate before cumulating, so gains and costs accruing at different dates are weighted consistently.
Because the spending increase is permanent, its fiscal cost continues to accumulate alongside its output effects.
The multiplier therefore converges only gradually toward its long-run value.

Permanent spending increases yield cumulative multipliers that vary across instruments, horizons, and country-group calibrations (Table~\ref{tab:multipliers}).
The spending instruments exhibit distinct dynamic profiles:
\begin{inparaenum}[(i)]
\item \emph{Government consumption} provides a natural reference point because it is the standard fiscal instrument.
It raises output from the outset by adding to demand.
Because it does not expand productive capacity, its multiplier remains around one-half over time.
\item \emph{Infrastructure investment}, by contrast, has a different profile.
Although its initial demand effect on output is similar to that of government consumption, larger gains emerge as productive public capital accumulates.
Over two decades, the cumulative multiplier expands severalfold.
\item \emph{Human-capital investment} has a strong short-run multiplier, supported by the initial increase in labor supply.
After some initial weakening, human-capital accumulation yields a large long-term multiplier, the highest among all instruments.
\item \emph{R\&D} starts more slowly because technologies must first be created and then adopted.
\end{inparaenum}
By the 25-year horizon, both have caught up with or overtaken infrastructure, and their advantage widens further in the long term.
At medium horizons, the productive-spending multipliers are broadly in line with empirical estimates of public-investment and R\&D multipliers \citep{Ramey2020, AfonsoRodrigues2024, FieldhouseMertens2023}.
The model's larger long-term values reflect the durability of the productive stocks these instruments create.

The differences across country groups reveal the role of productive capacity in the model.
The multiplier for government consumption, which relies on demand channels, is broadly similar across the two calibrations at short and medium horizons.
The infrastructure and human-capital multipliers are nearly twice as large in EMDEs as in AEs at medium-term horizons.
This difference is consistent with the higher elasticities calibrated for EMDEs, where public-capital stocks are less developed.
The absence of an EMDE R\&D multiplier reflects a different source of technology growth: adoption of existing technologies rather than endogenous creation driven by public R\&D.

\subsection{Improving Efficiency} \label{subsec:efficiency}

The efficiency wedges $e^{GI}$, $e^{GE}$, and $e^{GRD}$ determine how much of existing spending becomes productive.
Reducing a wedge converts waste into effective public investment or R\&D without raising the corresponding outlay.
Hence, it expands productive capacity without an additional budgetary cost.

Comparing permanent efficiency improvements with matched increases in productive spending reveals different transmission channels behind similar output effects (Figure~\ref{fig:efficiencyAE}).
Each efficiency improvement is calibrated to match the increase in effective productive inputs generated by a debt-financed permanent spending increase of 0.25 percent of GDP.\footnote{Matching a 1-percent-of-GDP increase would require negative efficiency gaps for some instruments; the smaller 0.25-percent-of-GDP experiment is therefore used for illustration.}
For instrument $x$, this requires eliminating waste equal to $(1-e^{x,SS})\times0.25$ percent of GDP.
For AEs, the infrastructure gap narrows by approximately 5.3 percentage points from an initial 35.9 percent and the human-capital gap by 12.0 points from 30.6 percent.
The R\&D gap narrows by 25.0 percentage points from an initial 39.9 percent.
For EMDEs, the infrastructure gap narrows by 3.0 percentage points from 40.6 percent and the human-capital gap by 8.4 points from 32.9 percent.
No EMDE R\&D response is reported because technology creation is inactive in that calibration.

The efficiency improvements produce output responses similar to those from the matched spending increases, but the underlying transmission differs.
In the model, they neither absorb additional resources nor require debt financing, thereby avoiding the resource and wealth effects that crowd out private consumption.
Unlike a spending increase, however, eliminating waste does not add directly to demand or induce the same increase in work.
These forces largely offset each other, yielding similar output gains.
The conventional spending multiplier is undefined because the reform raises output without increasing outlays; it therefore delivers a positive output gain at zero additional fiscal cost in the model.
The expanding tax base also strengthens the primary balance and lowers public debt.

Efficiency improvements yield larger gains in EMDEs, reflecting the same calibration features that amplify productive-spending effects: a higher output elasticity of infrastructure and a higher marginal product of public human capital.

\section{Conclusions} \label{sec:conclusions}
%========================================================================

This paper has documented a model that quantifies two distinct levers of public spending policy: the allocation of spending across categories and the efficiency with which spending is converted into productive capital.
By embedding expenditure-side fiscal policy and human capital formation in an endogenous growth framework, the model captures the different horizons over which infrastructure, human capital, and innovation pay off.
The simulations show that reallocating spending toward investment, and closing efficiency gaps, can raise output materially over the long run.
The gains are larger for emerging market and developing economies, which start further from the efficiency frontier and feature a higher marginal product of public capital.
The framework can be used to assess the long-run output consequences of country-specific spending reforms, and applications to individual economies are a natural next step.

\section{Analytical Derivations} \label{app:equilibrium}
%========================================================================

This appendix derives the model conditions that come from optimization problems, price-setting decisions, and technology-value recursions.
It covers the household problem, the production and pricing block, and the creation and adoption of technologies.
The conditions are written in the same notation as the main text, with the technology-adoption block stated in stationarized form as discussed in Subsection~\ref{subsec:bgp}.

\subsection{Households}

The representative household chooses $\{C_t, L_t, E_t, K_t, B_t, H_t\}_{t\ge 0}$ to maximize lifetime utility subject to the flow budget constraint, the private-capital law of motion, and the human-capital law of motion, all given in Section~\ref{sec:model}.
At each date, the inherited private stocks $K_{t-1}$ and $H_{t-1}$ are predetermined states, while the household chooses their next-period values through investment and education time.
It takes as given prices, taxes, transfers, rebated profits $\Omega_t$, the government-provided input into human-capital formation $K_{t-1}^{GE}$, and the trend $\Lambda_{t-1}$.
Substituting investment $I_t = K_t - (1-\delta) K_{t-1}$ and effective labor $N_t = H_{t-1} L_t$ out of the budget constraint and attaching the multiplier $\lambda_t$ to the budget constraint and $\lambda_t^{H}$ to the human-capital law of motion, the Lagrangian is
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t \Big\{\, & \ln C_t - \omega\,\frac{\left(L_t + E_t\right)^{1+\varphi}}{1+\varphi} \\[2pt]
& + \lambda_t \Big[ \left(1-\tau_t^{w}\right) w_t H_{t-1} L_t + r_t^{k} K_{t-1} + \tfrac{R_{t-1}}{\Pi_t} B_{t-1} + T_t + \Omega_t \\
& \qquad\;\; - \left(1+\tau_t^{c}\right) C_t - \big(K_t - (1-\delta) K_{t-1}\big) - B_t \Big] \\[2pt]
& + \lambda_t^{H} \Big[ \left(1-\delta^{h}\right) H_{t-1} + \chi\, E_t^{\gamma} \big(K_{t-1}^{GE}/\Lambda_{t-1}\big)^{\mu} - H_t \Big] \Big\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The first-order conditions with respect to $C_t$, $B_t$, $K_t$, $L_t$, $E_t$, and $H_t$ are
\begin{align*}
[\,C_t\,]:&\quad \frac{1}{C_t} = \lambda_t \left(1+\tau_t^{c}\right), \\
[\,B_t\,]:&\quad \lambda_t = \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\lambda_{t+1}\, \frac{R_t}{\Pi_{t+1}}\right], \\
[\,K_t\,]:&\quad \lambda_t = \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\lambda_{t+1}\left(r_{t+1}^{k} + 1 - \delta\right)\right], \\
[\,L_t\,]:&\quad \omega\left(L_t + E_t\right)^{\varphi} = \lambda_t \left(1-\tau_t^{w}\right) w_t H_{t-1}, \\
[\,E_t\,]:&\quad \omega\left(L_t + E_t\right)^{\varphi} = \lambda_t^{H}\, \chi\gamma\, E_t^{\gamma-1} \big(K_{t-1}^{GE}/\Lambda_{t-1}\big)^{\mu}, \\
[\,H_t\,]:&\quad \lambda_t^{H} = \beta\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\lambda_{t+1}\left(1-\tau_{t+1}^{w}\right) w_{t+1} L_{t+1} + \lambda_{t+1}^{H}\left(1-\delta^{h}\right)\right].
\end{align*}
The conditions for $C_t$, $B_t$, and $K_t$ give the marginal utility of wealth and the bond and capital Euler equations.
Those for $L_t$, $E_t$, and $H_t$ are the labor-supply, education-time, and human-capital-value conditions, interpreted in Section~\ref{sec:model}.
Because work and schooling draw on the same time endowment, combining the labor-supply and education-time conditions balances the return to an hour of work against the return to an hour of schooling.

\subsection{Production and Technology}

In a textbook New Keynesian model, the monopolistically competitive firms that produce differentiated varieties are also the firms that set sticky prices.
This model separates those roles to keep two margins distinct.
Retailers carry the standard New Keynesian pricing margin: they differentiate output into varieties and set prices subject to Calvo frictions, taking real marginal cost as given.
Upstream intermediate producers carry the production and technology-valuation margin: they determine the real marginal cost faced by retailers and earn the markup profit flow that gives adopted technologies value.
The split lets public infrastructure, human capital, and endogenous technology affect production and technology values without changing the nominal pricing structure.

\paragraph{Final-Good Aggregation.}
A competitive final-good producer combines retail varieties through the CES aggregator
\[
    Y_t^{d} = \left[\int_0^1 y_t(i)^{(\epsilon-1)/\epsilon}\, di\right]^{\epsilon/(\epsilon-1)}.
\]
For a given $Y_t^{d}$, it chooses $y_t(i)$ to minimize expenditure $\int_0^1 P_t(i)\, y_t(i)\, di$.
The first-order condition delivers the variety demand
\[
    y_t(i) = \left(\frac{P_t(i)}{P_t}\right)^{-\epsilon} Y_t^{d},
\]
and zero profits give the price index
\[
    P_t = \left[\int_0^1 P_t(i)^{1-\epsilon}\, di\right]^{1/(1-\epsilon)}.
\]
This is the standard final-good block of the New Keynesian model \citep{gali2015}.

\paragraph{Price-Setting Firms.}
Retailers are the model's price-setting firms.
They purchase intermediate output at the relative price $mc_t$, transform it one-for-one into differentiated varieties, and set prices \`a la Calvo.
With one-for-one transformation, $mc_t$ is the retailer's real marginal cost.

The intermediate producers' markup rule below pins down this cost, so the Calvo problem is the standard New Keynesian pricing problem.
Concretely, each period, a fraction $1-\theta_p$ of retailers reoptimizes, while the remainder index their price to past inflation at rate $\chi_p$.
A reoptimizing retailer chooses the reset price $\Pi_t^{*}$ to maximize expected discounted profits while its price remains in force.
The optimality condition can be written recursively in a standard manner as
\begin{align*}
    x_{1,t} &= \lambda_t\, mc_t\, Y_t^{d} + \beta\theta_p\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\left(\frac{\Pi_t^{\chi_p}}{\Pi_{t+1}}\right)^{-\epsilon} x_{1,t+1}\right], \\
    x_{2,t} &= \lambda_t\, \Pi_t^{*}\, Y_t^{d} + \beta\theta_p\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\left(\frac{\Pi_t^{\chi_p}}{\Pi_{t+1}}\right)^{1-\epsilon} \frac{\Pi_t^{*}}{\Pi_{t+1}^{*}} x_{2,t+1}\right], \\
    \epsilon\, x_{1,t} &= (\epsilon-1)\, x_{2,t}.
\end{align*}
The auxiliary $x_{1,t}$ discounts marginal cost, $x_{2,t}$ discounts marginal revenue, and the last condition sets the reset price as a markup over marginal cost.
Aggregating over reoptimizing and indexing retailers gives the price law of motion
\[
    1 = \theta_p\left(\frac{\Pi_{t-1}^{\chi_p}}{\Pi_t}\right)^{1-\epsilon} + (1-\theta_p)\left(\Pi_t^{*}\right)^{1-\epsilon}.
\]
The same aggregation gives the price-dispersion wedge
\[
    v_t^{p}
    = \theta_p\left(\frac{\Pi_{t-1}^{\chi_p}}{\Pi_t}\right)^{-\epsilon} v_{t-1}^{p}
    + \left(1-\theta_p\right)\left(\Pi_t^{*}\right)^{-\epsilon}.
\]
This wedge maps final demand into gross output, $Y_t = v_t^{p}Y_t^{d}$, in the market-clearing condition.

\paragraph{Production and Marginal Cost.}
Intermediate producer $i$ uses private capital and effective labor, taking public infrastructure and adopted technology as external production shifters:
\[
    Y_t(i) = A_{t-1}^{\vartheta-1}\left(K_{t-1}^{GI}\right)^{\alpha_G} K_{t-1}(i)^{\alpha} N_t(i)^{1-\alpha}.
\]

They rent private capital and hire effective labor competitively.
Taking factor prices as given, a producer chooses $K_{t-1}(i)$ and $N_t(i)$ to produce a given level of output at minimum factor cost $r_t^{k} K_{t-1}(i) + w_t N_t(i)$.
The multiplier on the output constraint is unit factor cost under constant returns to the two chosen inputs.

In this layered production structure, intermediate producers sell to retailers at the fixed gross markup $\mu^{p}$ over unit factor cost; the transaction price is $mc_t$, so unit factor cost is $mc_t/\mu^{p}$.
After imposing symmetry across intermediate producers, the first-order conditions with respect to private capital and effective labor are
\begin{align*}
    [\,K_{t-1}\,]:&\quad r_t^{k} = \alpha\, \frac{mc_t}{\mu^{p}}\, \frac{Y_t}{K_{t-1}}, \\
    [\,N_t\,]:&\quad w_t = (1-\alpha)\, \frac{mc_t}{\mu^{p}}\, \frac{Y_t}{N_t}.
\end{align*}
Dividing the two conditions gives the common input ratio
\[
    \frac{K_{t-1}}{N_t} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\, \frac{w_t}{r_t^{k}},
\]
which is common across firms, since all face the same factor prices and technology.

Factor payments absorb $mc_t/\mu^{p}$ of revenue per unit of output.
Aggregate residual profits are revenue at the transaction price minus factor cost:
\[
    mc_t Y_t - \frac{mc_t}{\mu^{p}}Y_t
    = \frac{\mu^{p}-1}{\mu^{p}}\, mc_t\, Y_t.
\]
These profits accrue to the owners of adopted technologies and are the dividends priced in the value functions below.

\paragraph{Technology Creation and Adoption.}
Following \citet{CominGertler2006} and \citet{Anzoateguietal2019}, the model distinguishes between the stock of created technologies $Z_t$, which are available for adoption, and the stock of adopted technologies $A_t$, which enter production.
The gap $Z_t-A_t$ is the stock of unadopted technologies.

Technology creation is a reduced-form process: lagged human capital captures learning by doing, as in \citet{ChangGomesSchorfheide2002}, while lagged effective public R\&D expands the stock of technologies available for adoption:
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \log\!\left(\frac{Z_t}{Z^{SS}}\right) ={}& \rho_A \log\!\left(\frac{Z_{t-1}}{Z^{SS}}\right)
    + \left(1-\rho_A\right)\alpha_{HA} \log\!\left(\frac{H_{t-1}}{H^{SS}}\right) \\
    &{}+ \left(1-\rho_A\right)\alpha_{RD} \log\!\left(\frac{\left(1-e_{t-1}^{GRD}\right) G_{t-1}^{RD}}{\left(1-e^{GRD}\right) G^{RD,SS}}\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
Only the productive fraction $1-e_{t-1}^{GRD}$ of public R\&D enters creation; raw R\&D spending enters the government budget.\footnote{Because the creation equation is written in ratios to steady state, the common trend cancels from this expression.}

Each unadopted technology is adopted next period with probability
\begin{equation}
    q_t = \left(q_0 + \varepsilon_t^{q}\right)\left(S_t\right)^{\varsigma}, \label{eq:probadopt}
\end{equation}
increasing in adoption expenditure $S_t$ with elasticity $\varsigma$; the shock $\varepsilon_t^{q}$ shifts the adoption probability directly and is nonzero only in the technology-diffusion experiments.\footnote{In equation~\eqref{eq:probadopt}, $S_t$ is already expressed relative to the trend, so this is the stationarized form of $q_t = q_0\left(S_t/\Lambda_t\right)^{\varsigma}$ in Section~\ref{sec:model}.}

The adoption law of motion is
\begin{equation}
    \left(1+\gamma^{a}\right) A_{t} = q_t\, \phi\left(Z_{t-1} - A_{t-1}\right) + \phi A_{t-1}. \label{eq:Astat}
\end{equation}
The first term is the flow of surviving unadopted technologies that become adopted; the second is the surviving stock of technologies already in production.
The survival rate $\phi$ applies to both pools: an unadopted idea must survive before it can be converted into an adopted technology.\footnote{Equation~\eqref{eq:Astat} is stated in stationarized form. The factor $1+\gamma^{a}$ appears on the current stock because $Z_t$ and $A_t$ are detrended by the technology trend, not by the output trend; see Subsection~\ref{subsec:bgp}.}

An adopted technology entitles its owner to a stream of profits.
The aggregate residual profits derived above are shared equally among the $A_{t-1}$ technologies in production, so each adopted technology pays the per-technology flow
\[
    \pi_t^{a} \equiv \frac{\mu^{p}-1}{\mu^{p}}\, \frac{mc_t\, Y_t}{A_{t-1}}.
\]
The owner discounts future profit flows with the household stochastic discount factor $\mathit{SDF}_{t+1} = \beta\, \lambda_{t+1}\left(1+\tau_{t+1}^{c}\right)/[\lambda_t\left(1+\tau_t^{c}\right)]$.\footnote{For multi-period payoffs, write $\mathit{SDF}_{t,t+s}\equiv\prod_{\ell=1}^{s}\mathit{SDF}_{t+\ell}$, with $\mathit{SDF}_{t,t}=1$.}
The value $v_t$ is the discounted rent stream
\[
    v_t = \mathbb{E}_t \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} \phi^{s}\,\mathit{SDF}_{t,t+s}\,\pi_{t+s}^{a},
\]
so, equivalently,
\[
    v_t = \pi_t^{a} + \phi\,\mathbb{E}_t[\mathit{SDF}_{t+1}v_{t+1}].
\]

An unadopted technology has value from the possibility of future adoption, net of the adoption effort costs paid while waiting.
Let $Q_{t,s}\equiv\prod_{k=0}^{s-1}(1-q_{t+k})$ be the probability that a technology unadopted at $t$ is still unadopted at $t+s$, conditional on survival, with $Q_{t,0}=1$.
The variable $j_t$ denotes the value of an unadopted technology evaluated at the optimal adoption-effort policy:
\[
    j_t =
    -\mathbb{E}_t \sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\phi^{s}\,\mathit{SDF}_{t,t+s}\,Q_{t,s}\,s_{t+s}
    +\mathbb{E}_t \sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\phi^{s+1}\,\mathit{SDF}_{t,t+s+1}\,Q_{t,s}\,q_{t+s}\,v_{t+s+1}.
\]
The first term is the expected present value of per-technology adoption costs $s_{t+s}$ paid while the technology remains unadopted; the second is the expected present value of the adopted-technology value $v_{t+s+1}$ received when adoption occurs at $t+s$.
Splitting off the current-period cost and next-period continuation value gives the recursive representation
\[
    j_t = -s_t + \phi\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\mathit{SDF}_{t+1}\left(q_t v_{t+1} + (1-q_t)j_{t+1}\right)\right].
\]

The model carries aggregate rather than per-technology objects, so the unit recursions are scaled by the adopted stock active at $t$, $A_{t-1}$.
Define $\mathcal{V}_t \equiv A_{t-1}v_t$, $\mathcal{J}_t \equiv A_{t-1}j_t$, and $S_t \equiv A_{t-1}s_t$.
Using the aggregate-profit identity $A_{t-1}\pi_t^{a} = \frac{\mu^{p}-1}{\mu^{p}}\, mc_t\, Y_t$, the scaled value recursions are\footnote{Because the continuation values are scaled by the next period's stock ($\mathcal{V}_{t+1} = A_t v_{t+1}$), the ratio $A_{t-1}/A_t$ converts them back to the date-$t$ scaling $A_{t-1}$. The additional factor $1/(1+\gamma^{a})$ is the technology-growth correction associated with this stationarized scaling.}
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{V}_t &= \frac{\mu^{p}-1}{\mu^{p}}\, mc_t\, Y_t + \phi\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\mathit{SDF}_{t+1}\, \mathcal{V}_{t+1}\, \frac{A_{t-1}}{A_{t}}\, \frac{1}{1+\gamma^{a}}\right], \label{eq:VA}\\
    \mathcal{J}_t &= -S_t + \phi\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\mathit{SDF}_{t+1}\, \frac{A_{t-1}}{A_{t}}\, \frac{1}{1+\gamma^{a}}\left(q_t\, \mathcal{V}_{t+1} + \left(1-q_t\right)\mathcal{J}_{t+1}\right)\right]. \label{eq:JZ}
\end{align}
The adoption-probability function in equation~\eqref{eq:probadopt} implies $\partial q_t/\partial S_t = \left(q_0+\varepsilon_t^{q}\right)\varsigma S_t^{\varsigma-1} = \varsigma q_t/S_t$.
Choosing $S_t$ to maximize $\mathcal{J}_t$ then gives the adoption optimality condition
\begin{equation}
    S_t = \varsigma\, q_t\, \phi\, \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\mathit{SDF}_{t+1}\, \frac{1}{1+\gamma^{a}}\, \frac{A_{t-1}}{A_{t}}\left(\mathcal{V}_{t+1} - \mathcal{J}_{t+1}\right)\right], \label{eq:SAfoc}
\end{equation}
which equates the marginal cost of adoption effort---one unit of the good---to its marginal value: the probability gain $\varsigma\, q_t/S_t$ times the discounted, survival-adjusted adoption surplus.

\section{Government Consumption: Robustness to Model Structure} \label{app:simplified}
%========================================================================

This appendix examines which features of the model shape the transmission of a permanent government-consumption increase, with particular attention to inflation.
Figure~\ref{fig:simplifiedGc} traces the same increase used in Figure~\ref{fig:standardShocks} through seven cumulative simplifications of the full model.
The first three remove endogenous technology creation and adoption, human-capital accumulation, and productive public infrastructure, respectively.
The fourth removes private capital and replaces the factor-demand system with labor-only constant-returns production.
The next two eliminate price indexation and trend growth.
The final step replaces the extended fiscal block with wasteful government consumption financed by an implicit lump-sum tax.

The terminal specification contains the canonical New Keynesian household, production, Calvo-pricing, monetary-policy, and resource-constraint equations.
To verify the nesting, the figure also reports an independently coded canonical benchmark with the same parameters and shock.
The derived and independently coded responses coincide to numerical precision under both permanent and temporary spending increases.

Output rises in every specification, but the inflation response depends on the private-capital adjustment.
In the full model and the variants with active capital accumulation, investment and real marginal cost rise on impact, producing positive inflation.
Once private capital and its factor-demand conditions are removed, real marginal cost and inflation remain at their steady-state values.
The subsequent removal of indexation and trend growth therefore does not alter price dynamics in this permanent-shock sequence.
Appendix~\ref{app:tempshocks} repeats the simplification exercise for a persistent temporary increase.

\section{Persistent Temporary Shocks} \label{app:tempshocks}
%========================================================================

The headline exercises in Section~\ref{sec:properties} use permanent spending increases, consistent with the policy reforms in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
This appendix reports the corresponding results for persistent but temporary expansions.
Each shock raises one spending instrument by 1 percent of GDP on impact and then decays with AR(1) persistence of 0.9.

\subsection{Transmission}

Figure~\ref{fig:standardShocksTemp} traces the temporary counterparts of the four debt-financed expansions in Figure~\ref{fig:standardShocks} through the advanced-economy model.
Because spending gradually returns to its steady-state level, the productive stocks accumulate by less than under a permanent increase.
Short-run demand effects are correspondingly more prominent relative to the long-run supply response.

\subsection{Robustness to Model Structure}

Figure~\ref{fig:simplifiedGcTemp} repeats the model-simplification exercise of Figure~\ref{fig:simplifiedGc} for the temporary government-consumption shock.
Output remains positive across the full model and all seven cumulative simplifications.
Inflation is also positive in every specification, including the labor-only and canonical endpoints in which it is zero under the permanent increase.
At the canonical endpoint, the temporary increase produces a weaker negative wealth effect on labor supply, allowing the demand effect to raise the real wage, marginal cost, and inflation.
The response of these benchmarks shows that the duration of the spending increase can change inflation dynamics independently of the model's growth and capital channels.
The derived endpoint again coincides with the independently coded canonical control.

Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivityTemp} repeats the sensitivity exercise of Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} for the persistent temporary shocks.

\subsection{Fiscal Multipliers}

Table~\ref{tab:multipliersTemp} reports the present-value cumulative multipliers for the persistent temporary shocks.
Once the spending increase fades, the denominator of the cumulative multiplier stops growing rapidly, while the output effects of the productive stocks dissipate more slowly.
The temporary-shock multipliers can therefore exceed their permanent counterparts at intermediate horizons.
At 25 years, for example, the advanced-economy human-capital multiplier is 3.3 under the temporary shock, compared with 1.7 under the permanent increase.
